# Funding report for Winnie Kalisa

Research date: 27 October 2026. Data file: `data/opportunities/funding.json` (17 entries, ids start with `fund-`). Every entry was checked on its official page or official PDF. Amounts, deadlines and eligibility are quoted as those pages state them. Where a page did not give a figure, the entry says so.

The situation this report covers: Winnie is a Rwandan citizen moving to San Francisco on a visa that does not let her work in the US. She is already applying for a grant for a project in Rwanda.

**This is not legal advice.** The rules below are the general ones, with links to the official text. Whether a particular payment is allowed depends on her exact visa status and the facts of each payment. She should talk to an immigration attorney before she accepts any money while she is in the US, and before she changes status.

## 1. Visa context, in plain language

### What counts as work

- **Unauthorized work breaks status.** A nonimmigrant may only do the work her status allows, and "any unauthorized employment by a nonimmigrant constitutes a failure to maintain status" ([8 CFR 214.1(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-214/section-214.1)).
- **Employment is defined by where the work is done.** Federal regulations define employment as "any service or labor performed by an employee for an employer within the United States" ([8 CFR 274a.1(h)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-274a/subpart-A/section-274a.1)). That definition is written for employer-verification rules, so don't read it as a general permission. Also, freelance or self-employed work done while she is physically in the US is generally treated as work in the US, even if the client is in Rwanda. Ask the attorney about this directly.
- **Freelance fees, teaching fees, commission payments and studio-assistant wages** earned while in the US are the clearest cases of work that needs authorization. In this dataset that covers the ACTA mentor contract and Clay Art Center's paid studio hours.

### What is often treated differently, and still needs checking

- **Honoraria (only for B visitors).** The statute lets someone admitted on a B visitor visa accept an honorarium and incidental expenses for "a usual academic activity" lasting no more than 9 days at any single institution, from no more than 5 institutions in 6 months. The payer must be a nonprofit or academic institution or organisation of the kind the statute lists ([INA 212(q), 8 U.S.C. 1182(q)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/8/1182)). This exception is tied to B status. If Winnie is on a dependent visa (such as H-4, F-2 or L-2), it does not automatically apply to her.
- **Grants and awards for work done outside the US.** The IRS treats "scholarships, fellowship grants, targeted grants, and achievement awards received by nonresident aliens for activities performed, or to be performed, outside the United States" as not US-source income ([IRS Publication 519](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519)). Together with the "within the United States" definition above, this is why grants for her Rwanda project, paid for work done in Rwanda, are generally the safest kind of money. That is a tax rule, though, not an immigration ruling. The attorney should confirm how her status treats them, especially for any part of the work she does from San Francisco.
- **Grants paid to an institution.** When the money goes to an organisation (for example a Rwandan NGO under UNESCO's IFCD, or a US university hosting her as a J-1 scholar), the organisation is the grantee. Her own role and any pay to her still have to fit her status.
- **Prizes.** I found no USCIS page that classifies a competition prize, like Mino or LOEWE, as employment or as not employment. A prize for a finished work entered from abroad is commonly viewed as different from wages, but she should have the attorney confirm this before she accepts one while living in the US. Prize money may also be taxed (the Mino guidelines say so).
- **Dependent work permits.** The rules depend on her status. A J-2 spouse "is entitled to employment authorization" by filing Form I-765, though the J-2's income cannot be used to support the J-1 ([USCIS: Exchange Visitors](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/students-and-exchange-visitors/exchange-visitors)). An H-4 spouse can get an EAD only in specific cases, such as when the H-1B spouse has an approved I-140 or AC21 extension ([USCIS: H-4 EAD](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/temporary-workers/h-1b-specialty-occupations/employment-authorization-for-certain-h-4-dependent-spouses)). If a work permit becomes possible, the entries labelled `needs-work-authorization` open up.

### Visa routes that come with their own authorization

USCIS says J-1 work is allowed "only under the terms of the exchange program" ([USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/students-and-exchange-visitors/exchange-visitors)). A J-1 therefore covers the hosted activity, not general freelance work.

### Bay Area and US institutions with a documented J-1 route

- **UC Berkeley, Berkeley International Office.** Sponsors J-1 Research Scholars and Professors (21 days to 5 years) ([BIO](https://internationaloffice.berkeley.edu/node/191)). The **Center for African Studies** hosts visiting scholars, who are independent researchers with library access but no office space. You first email the Center with credentials, project, proposed faculty host and dates. International J-1 scholars must show funding (the CAS info sheet lists US$1,800 a month plus US$500 for a spouse; check BIO's current minimum) and pay university fees. Visas through Fulbright and other sponsoring programs are handled differently ([CAS visiting scholar information](https://africa.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/1.ucb_.cas_.visiting.scholar.information.pdf)). The natural pairing is the **Fulbright Visiting Scholar** award, which brings the stipend and the J-1, with a Berkeley CAS or art-practice faculty invitation.
- **Stanford, Bechtel International Center.** Sponsors J-1 scholars when a host department requests the visa document ([Bechtel J-1 Scholar Visa](https://bechtel.stanford.edu/navigate-international-life/visas/j-1-scholar-visa)).
- **Fulbright Visiting Scholar Program.** Scholars enter under the State Department's own J-1 sponsorship ([Fulbright Scholar Program](https://fulbrightscholars.org/non-us-scholars/fulbright-visiting-scholar-program)). The State Department says Rwandan scholars may apply ([ECA Rwanda page](https://2012-2025.eca.state.gov/fulbright/country/rwanda.html)).
- **Rijksakademie (Amsterdam, outside the US).** Explicitly offers "support with visa process in the Netherlands" ([Rijksakademie](https://rijksakademie.nl/en/residency-apply/apply)).
- **Residencies that welcome international artists but don't publish their visa route.** Kohler Arts/Industry, Winterthur, and the Bay Area residencies already in the dataset (Headlands, Montalvo, Djerassi). I found no statement on their sites about which visa international residents use. Ask each one before accepting, and have the attorney check that the visa they name fits her situation.
- To check whether any other host is a designated J-1 sponsor, use the "Find a Program Sponsor" search on [j1visa.state.gov](https://j1visa.state.gov/programs/research-scholar).

## 2. Best fits for Winnie, ranked

The ranking weighs how likely she is to qualify, how well it fits her practice (wheel and hand-building, wild clay, glaze chemistry, weaving, the Rwanda project), whether her visa allows it, and timing.

1. **International Ceramics Competition Mino** (`fund-mino-international-ceramics-competition`). Free, "open to everyone", judged from images, top prize JPY 5,000,000, deadline **29 January 2027**. It is a prize entered from anywhere, the strongest O-1B credential on this list, and the cheapest to try.
2. **Winterthur Maker-Creator Fellowship** (`fund-winterthur-maker-creator`). US$3,000 a month for 4-6 weeks with ceramics and textile collections, open to artists, due **15 January 2027** (optional draft review by 1 December). It fits her research side. Before accepting, confirm which visa Winterthur's international fellows use.
3. **UNESCO IFCD through a Rwandan partner** (`fund-unesco-ifcd`). Up to US$100,000 for a 12-24 month project in Rwanda. Rwanda is eligible, but only NGOs and public bodies can apply. This is the best way to scale up the Rwanda project she is already pursuing: a potters' cooperative or registered association applies, and she contributes the work from Rwanda. The next call is likely around March 2027; the 2026 call ran 23 March to 6 May.
4. **Kohler Arts/Industry** (`fund-kohler-arts-industry`). Three months in an industrial pottery with a US$5,000 stipend, housing, travel and materials, and "International artists are welcome". The 2027 cycle ran 1 January to 28 February 2026; watch for the 2028 dates. Ask what visa past international residents used.
5. **Fulbright Visiting Scholar plus a Berkeley host** (`fund-fulbright-visiting-scholar-rwanda`). Comes with its own J-1, a stipend in most cases, and travel. She applies through US Embassy Kigali's Public Affairs Section. Ask whether an artist-researcher qualifies and whether she must apply while resident in Rwanda.
6. **Prince Claus Seed Award** (`fund-prince-claus-seed-award`). EUR 5,000 unrestricted, and Rwanda is eligible, but only while she still lives and works there. If the 2027 call opens before she moves (the 2026 deadline was 8 January 2026), apply.
7. **LOEWE FOUNDATION Craft Prize** (`fund-loewe-foundation-craft-prize`). EUR 50,000; all nationalities; ceramics and textiles both qualify. The 2027 edition closed on 15 October 2026, so prepare one strong recent piece for the next call.
8. **Rijksakademie Residency** (`fund-rijksakademie-residency`). EUR 19,800 stipend (2026), EUR 2,500 work budget, Dutch visa support. The 2028 call opens **1 January 2027**. It is a big commitment away from San Francisco, but it is fully funded and open to "artists from all countries".
9. **Goethe-Institut AEPC mobility grants** (`fund-goethe-aepc-mobility`). Up to EUR 4,000 per trip within Africa or between Africa and Europe, for people based in Sub-Saharan Africa. Useful for research trips (for example to other East African clay traditions) before the move. Round III has closed; watch for a further round.
10. **FCA Emergency Grants** (`fund-fca-emergency-grants`). US$500 to US$3,000, rolling, for artists living in the US with a tax ID (an ITIN counts), and the show can be in Kigali. It needs a confirmed exhibition and experimental work, and an attorney check before she accepts money in the US.
11. **US Embassy Kigali Public Diplomacy grants** (`fund-us-embassy-kigali-pd-grants`). US$10,000 to US$35,000 in the FY25 notice, and individuals can apply. Arts were not a stated priority, so she would frame a proposal as craft livelihoods, skills training or US-Rwanda exchange.
12. **Already in the dataset, worth a look** (not re-verified for this report): `institut-francais-visas` (Visas pour la Création, for artists residing in Africa, listed with a **November** deadline; check it now), `african-arts-trust` (East African visual artists, rolling), `african-culture-fund`, `pollock-krasner-foundation` (rolling, international), `pottery-workshop-jingdezhen`, and `nceca-international`.
13. **For later, once she has a work permit or O-1** (`needs-work-authorization`): `fund-acta-apprenticeship` (US$5,000 mentor contract for teaching a traditional art in California) and `fund-creative-capital-award` (US$15,000 to US$50,000, open to O-1 holders).
